Digital Nomad Visa: Which Countries Offer One and How to Apply

Cansun Tanıl Tabakoğlu May 28, 2026 9 min read

The pandemic didn't just close doors. It also proved that millions of people could compress their entire working life into a laptop, a bag, and an internet connection. Out of that realization, a new travel document was born: the digital nomad visa.

Governments that recognized remote work as a permanent structural shift began offering this new class of globally mobile workers something they had long lacked: a legal status. A digital nomad visa grants individuals who earn their living remotely, working for employers or clients outside the host country, the explicit legal right to reside and work within its borders for a defined period.

The distinction from a tourist visa is fundamental. A tourist visa legally prohibits work in most jurisdictions, including, in strict interpretations, working remotely for a foreign employer while physically present in the country. A digital nomad visa removes that ambiguity and replaces it with a clear, lawful status. It allows you to live within a country's borders, contribute to its local economy through daily spending, and remain outside its income tax system, provided you don't establish tax residency there.

Why Countries Open This Door

The logic driving these programs is straightforward. Digital nomads spend money locally, on rent, food, transportation, and services, while their income arrives from another country. This is a particularly attractive profile for tourism-dependent economies: significant consumer spending with no corresponding demand on the public payroll or social services. For countries trying to attract high-income, educated residents without the long-term commitments of traditional immigration, digital nomad visas represent a structurally efficient solution.

Europe's Leading Programs

Portugal: D8 Visa

Launched in September 2022, Portugal's D8 visa made it one of the first major EU countries to introduce a dedicated digital nomad visa. Applicants must demonstrate remote work for an employer or clients outside Portugal and show monthly income of approximately four times the Portuguese minimum wage, around €3,680 as of 2026 (four times the Portuguese minimum wage of €920), a figure updated annually; verify against current official sources before applying. A one-year visa can be renewed; after meeting presence requirements over several years, the path toward permanent residency and eventually citizenship opens.

Portugal had previously been exceptionally attractive through its NHR (Non-Habitual Resident) tax regime, which offered significant benefits on certain foreign-sourced income for a decade. As of 2024, NHR was closed entirely to new applicants. Its replacement, IFICI (Tax Incentive for Scientific Research and Innovation), is a narrowly targeted instrument aimed at researchers, approved technology specialists, and entrepreneurs in specific sectors. IFICI does not apply to general digital nomads. If tax advantages are a key reason you are considering Portugal, verify whether you qualify under IFICI through a qualified tax advisor before making any assumptions.

Spain: Startups Law Visa

Spain's dedicated digital nomad visa, introduced under the January 2023 Startups Law, requires applicants to have been working remotely for a non-Spanish company or client for at least three months and to demonstrate monthly income of approximately twice Spain's minimum wage (updated to €2,849/month as of March 2026, linked to 200% of Spain's minimum interprofessional salary of €1,221; verify through official sources). The visa is initially valid for one year with two-year renewals possible, up to five years in total. A notable feature is the "Beckham Law": a flat 24% income tax rate available for the first six years for qualifying individuals.

Croatia

Croatia launched its digital nomad temporary residence permit in January 2021, making it one of Europe's first movers in this space. Available to non-EU nationals, the permit allows stays of up to one year. The income requirement stands at approximately €3,295/month as of 2026. It is worth noting that Croatia became a full Schengen Area member in January 2023, granting holders freedom of movement across the Schengen zone. Croatia also offers a notable tax advantage: foreign-sourced income earned under the digital nomad permit is fully exempt from Croatian income tax. A critical feature distinguishes it from other programs: it is non-renewable. When the permit expires, you must leave Croatia before reapplying. This effectively positions Croatia as a short-term base rather than a long-term relocation option.

Greece

Greece's digital nomad visa, introduced in 2021, grants a one-year residence permit (renewable for one additional year) for applicants who can demonstrate remote work for non-Greek employers or clients and a monthly income of at least €3,500. The program's most compelling feature is its tax incentive: individuals who were not tax residents of Greece in the prior five years are eligible to have 50% of their taxable income base excluded from calculation for seven years. This reduction applies to all income earned in Greece, not exclusively to foreign-sourced income. Important procedural update (February 2026): Applications can no longer be submitted in-country on a tourist visa. Applicants must now apply for a one-year digital nomad visa through a Greek consulate in their home country first, then convert it to a residence permit after arrival in Greece. This positions Greece as one of Europe's most tax-efficient digital nomad destinations for medium-term planning.

Estonia

Estonia launched its digital nomad visa in August 2020, an early mover in what would become a global trend. The monthly income requirement stands at €4,500 gross as of 2026. A common point of confusion: Estonia also operates the widely known e-Residency program, which is entirely separate. E-Residency allows non-residents to establish and manage a company within the EU's digital infrastructure; it confers no physical residency rights. The digital nomad visa grants the right to actually live in Estonia. The two should not be conflated.

Beyond Europe

United Arab Emirates (Dubai)

Dubai launched a one-year remote work visa in 2021 requiring applicants to demonstrate employment or self-employment with a monthly income of approximately USD 5,000, alongside valid health insurance. While the application fee is relatively modest, the total cost including health insurance and associated documentation typically reaches a more significant sum. The UAE's position as a global transit and business hub adds practical appeal beyond the visa itself.

Thailand: LTR Visa

Thailand restructured its Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa program in 2022. Under the "Work-from-Thailand Professional" category, individuals working remotely for a non-Thai company with annual income of at least USD 80,000 are eligible for a 10-year residence permit. The program includes additional tax advantages and streamlined bureaucratic processes, making it one of the region's most substantive long-term offerings for remote professionals.

Costa Rica

Costa Rica's digital nomad visa, launched in August 2022, requires monthly income of USD 3,000 for individuals or USD 4,000 for families. The two-year visa can be renewed once. A notable benefit: foreign-sourced income is not subject to Costa Rican income tax under this program, making the country's financial environment particularly straightforward for remote earners.

Barbados: Welcome Stamp

Barbados was among the first countries in the world to launch a dedicated remote work program, introducing its Welcome Stamp in July 2020. Applicants must demonstrate annual income of at least USD 50,000 and hold valid health insurance. The stamp grants one year of legal residence. The combination of a transparent application process, English as the official language, and a genuinely attractive physical environment made Barbados one of the most visible early entrants in this space.

The Tax Dimension: The Most Overlooked Variable

This is, without question, the issue most digital nomads encounter too late. Living in a country and being a tax resident of that country are not the same thing, but they can become the same thing faster than most people expect. Most countries define tax residency by a single criterion: spending more than 183 days per calendar year within their borders. Cross that threshold, and the local tax system may apply to your global income, regardless of where it originates.

Individuals spending time across multiple countries simultaneously face the risk of triggering tax obligations in two jurisdictions at once. Tax treaties between countries mitigate this in many cases, but for combinations of countries without bilateral agreements, the situation can become genuinely complex. Engaging a qualified international tax advisor before making any significant relocation decision is not a precaution reserved for the wealthy; it is basic financial due diligence.

Traveling freely is a privilege. But building the legal and financial infrastructure to do so sustainably requires far more thought than geography alone.

What You'll Generally Need to Apply

While requirements vary significantly by country, most digital nomad visa programs converge on a similar set of core documentation: proof of remote employment or freelance contracts; three to six months of bank statements demonstrating sufficient income; comprehensive health insurance valid for the duration of your stay; a clean criminal record certificate; and a passport with sufficient remaining validity.

Some countries accept applications through their consulates abroad; others require certain steps to be completed in-country. Before committing to any destination, verify the specific application process through the country's official immigration authority.

What to Consider Before You Apply

Program requirements evolve. The Portuguese NHR reform and threshold changes across multiple countries in recent years illustrate how quickly the landscape can shift. Base your decisions on current official sources, not on blog posts, including this one, that may have been written before the latest changes.

Your employer's position matters as much as the country's rules. Some companies are reluctant to accommodate employees working from another country, citing the legal and tax complications that physical presence abroad can create. Clarify your employer's policy, and your contract, before making any commitments.

Finally, working on a tourist visa in a country that prohibits it is a legal violation in most jurisdictions. The digital nomad visa exists precisely to resolve that ambiguity. Operating without the appropriate legal status may appear practical in the short term; in the longer term, it carries risks that are difficult to predict and hard to recover from.

The border crossings haven't changed physically. But the conditions for crossing them have never been more varied, or more legally accessible, than they are today.
Note: The information in this article is provided for general informational purposes only. Digital nomad visa requirements, income thresholds, and application processes change frequently. Before making any application, we strongly recommend consulting the official immigration authority of the relevant country and seeking advice from a qualified immigration and tax professional.
