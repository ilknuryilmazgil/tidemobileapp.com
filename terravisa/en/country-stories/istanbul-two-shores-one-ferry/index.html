Two Shores, One Ferry: My Istanbul Route from İstiklal to Moda

From St. Anthony’s and the Flower Passage to the Crimean Memorial Church, Dolmabahçe and a ferry to Kadıköy and Moda: the Istanbul route I walked in October 2026.

By: Cansun Tanıl TabakoğluNovember 26, 20265 min read

This story was translated from Turkish by TerraVisa.

Wandering around Istanbul in October 2026, my route ended up tying the city’s two shores together on its own. It started on İstiklal, ran through the streets of Galata down to Dolmabahçe, and from there crossed by ferry to Kadıköy and Moda.

When I turned onto İstiklal, the avenue was as crowded as ever. The red tram was crawling through the people. I only read later that this street used to be called Cadde-i Kebir, the Grand Avenue, and that Europeans knew it as the Grande Rue de Péra. At one point I turned into a side street and looked up. The buildings were so tall and the street so narrow that the sky was just a thin strip overhead. I still have that shot on my phone.

I walked past Galatasaray High School. The school’s story goes back to 1481. A little further on is the Church of St. Anthony of Padua. Its red-brick façade stops you in the middle of the avenue. It was built between 1906 and 1912 by the architect Giulio Mongeri and is Istanbul’s largest Catholic church. Once I stepped inside, the noise of the street was gone and coloured light from the stained glass was falling across the pews. Angelo Roncalli, who later became Pope John XXIII, is said to have preached here while he was the Vatican’s envoy in Istanbul.

Right next to Galatasaray, I took a break in Çiçek Pasajı, the Flower Passage. I sat at one of the tables under the glass roof and ordered a beer. After a while the live music started and the whole passage changed. Outside the door, İstiklal’s crowd kept flowing past; inside, time seemed to move at a different speed. The passage opened in 1876 as the Cité de Péra, and it is said to take its present name from the Russian émigré women who sold flowers here in the 1920s.

From there I headed down towards Galata. In one of the streets just behind the tower, on Serdar-ı Ekrem, I came across a grey stone church. I didn’t know its name that day; I found out later: the Crimean Memorial Church. It was built in memory of the British soldiers who died in the Crimean War, on land given by Sultan Abdülmecid, and opened for worship in 1868. Inside, candles were burning beneath the rose window. After the crowds of Galata, the few minutes I spent there were the quietest of the whole trip.

Then I went down to the shore. Dolmabahçe Palace stretches along the water with its long white façade. It was built between 1843 and 1856, under Sultan Abdülmecid, by architects of the Balyan family. But the real weight of this palace isn’t its decoration: Atatürk died here on 10 November 1938, at 9:05 in the morning. Walking past, you can’t help thinking about it. Keep walking and you reach Beşiktaş; beside the pier stands the tomb of Barbaros Hayreddin Pasha, built by Mimar Sinan, and right behind it the noise of the market begins.

Then I got on the ferry, under a deep blue sky. As we got closer to Kadıköy, Haydarpaşa Station appeared at the water’s edge. I took a photo of it past the ferry’s white post; that photo is the cover of this story. Towards evening I was on a ferry again, but this time the sky had clouded over. In the last light coming through the clouds, the city looked completely different.

Kadıköy’s old name is Chalcedon, founded by colonists from Megara in the 7th century BC. It has a legend too: the founders of Byzantium were told to settle “opposite the blind”, because the Chalcedonians had overlooked the better peninsula across the water and settled here instead. That is where the district’s nickname, the “Land of the Blind”, comes from. Today’s Kadıköy, with its fishmongers, coffee shops and bookshops, feels less like a neighbourhood than a small city.

From the market it isn’t a long walk to Moda. Stand at Moda Point facing the sea and the European side stretches out in front of you. That’s the nice thing about seeing both shores on the same trip: when you look across, you know you were just over there.

A small note if you go: get an İstanbulkart first, the same card works on ferries and trams. Dolmabahçe’s visiting hours can change, so check the National Palaces website before you go. The churches are places of worship; keep quiet inside and ask before taking photos.
