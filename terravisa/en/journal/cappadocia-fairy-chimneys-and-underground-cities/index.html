Cappadocia: Fairy Chimneys, Rock Churches and Underground Cities

The story of Cappadocia’s fairy chimneys, its rock-cut churches and the underground cities of Kaymaklı and Derinkuyu, based on UNESCO sources.

November 5, 20265 min readLast checked: November 12, 2026

First the volcanoes spoke. Then erosion patiently carved the land they left behind. In the landscape that emerged, sharp rock pillars reach for the sky, churches hide inside the rock, and cities stretch beneath the ground. This is Cappadocia. UNESCO inscribed it on the World Heritage List in 1985 as “Göreme National Park and the Rock Sites of Cappadocia”.

In this article we explore Cappadocia in three layers: on the surface, inside the rock and underground.

Layer one: the land of fairy chimneys

Cappadocia lies on the central Anatolian plateau, in a volcanic landscape sculpted by erosion into a succession of ridges, valleys and rock pinnacles known as “fairy chimneys”.

Look towards the horizon and you will see the leading actors of this story. Ranges of extinct volcanoes bound the area to the south and east, with Mount Erciyes (3,916 m) at one end and Mount Hasan (3,253 m) at the other.

According to UNESCO, the Göreme Valley and its surroundings are a globally renowned and accessible display of fairy chimney landforms. In other words, this landscape is not hidden away somewhere remote and out of reach.

Layer two: life inside the rock

What turns Cappadocia from a geological wonder into something more is the bond people built with its rock. Rock-hewn cells, churches, troglodyte villages… According to UNESCO, their density makes the region one of the world’s most striking and largest cave-dwelling complexes.

The story goes back to the 4th century. UNESCO notes that the first signs of monastic life in the region are believed to date from this period, when small communities of hermits following the teachings of Basil the Great, Bishop of Kayseri, began living in cells cut into the rock.

After 842, the inside of the rock filled with colour. Many rock churches were dug and richly decorated with brightly coloured figurative painting. Because of this decoration, UNESCO describes Cappadocia as one of the leading examples of post-iconoclastic Byzantine art.

Did you know? The rock churches of the Göreme Valley greet you from different centuries. According to UNESCO, Tokalı Kilise and El Nazar Kilise date from the 10th century, St Barbara Kilise and Saklı Kilise from the 11th, and Elmalı Kilise and Karanlık Kilise from the end of the 12th to the beginning of the 13th century.

Layer three: cities beneath the ground

Perhaps Cappadocia’s most surprising secret lies beneath your feet. According to UNESCO, in later periods communities gathered in troglodyte villages and in underground towns such as Kaymaklı and Derinkuyu to resist Arab raids. These towns were places of refuge. Today both are part of the World Heritage property.

Picture it: valleys above, life below. In Cappadocia, history is not only beside you but under you too.

Cappadocia on the map

The World Heritage property covers the area between Nevşehir, Ürgüp and Avanos, the sites of Karain, Karlık, Yeşilöz and Soğanlı, and the underground cities of Kaymaklı and Derinkuyu.

UNESCO stresses that Cappadocia is a traditional settlement made vulnerable by the combined effects of natural erosion and tourism. So that this landscape survives for future generations, stay on marked paths and avoid anything that could damage the rock formations.
