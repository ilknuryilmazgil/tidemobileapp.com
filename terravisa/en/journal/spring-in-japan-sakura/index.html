Spring in Japan: Cherry Blossom Season

When the cherry blossoms open across Japan, where the hanami tradition comes from, the best-known viewing spots and the etiquette to follow.

November 10, 20266 min readLast checked: November 12, 2026

In Japan, spring doesn’t arrive in a day. It travels the length of the country. The first cherry blossoms open in Okinawa in mid-January, and the last say goodbye in Hokkaido at the end of May. In the months between, a pink wave moves from south to north, and every year people gather to watch it pass.

So what makes one flower so special? According to the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO), sakura are tied to Japan’s history, culture and identity, and over time they came to stand for impermanence, hope and renewal. The blossoms start to fall at the very peak of their beauty, and they leave a quiet reminder: life is beautiful, but it is short.

Where to catch the pink wave

The sakura calendar is less a date than a route. The season starts in the south and climbs north over the weeks. According to JNTO, trees bloom across most of Honshu between March and May, but where you are, and when, decides what you will see:

  • Okinawa, mid-January: The season opens with Hikan-zakura, whose bright-pink blossoms tend to face downwards.
  • Kawazu (Shizuoka), late February to late March: Kawazu-zakura is one of the first varieties to herald spring on Honshu.
  • Across Honshu, March and April: The stage belongs to Somei Yoshino, the best-known variety.
  • Tohoku, early May: At some spots, the blossoms are only just waking up.
  • Hokkaido, late April to late May: Yoshino cherry blossoms are the last stop on the journey.

A tip: full-bloom dates change from year to year. Check JNTO’s latest cherry blossom forecasts before you book; following them improves your chances of seeing the trees at their fullest.

Hanami: where time slows down

In Japanese, “hana” means flower and “mi” means to view. Hanami, “flower viewing”, sounds simple, but it names a gathering that goes back a long way.

There are several theories about how it began. According to JNTO, the most popular is that aristocrats recited poems while viewing flowers in the Nara period (710–794). Interestingly, back then most eyes were on the plum blossom, not the cherry. The cherry found its way to becoming the symbol of spring over time.

Did you know? According to one theory cited by JNTO, the “sa” in “sakura” refers to the gods of rice fields and harvest, and “kura” means “a place where the gods reside”. On this view, farmers celebrated hanami to pray for a good harvest as the rice-planting season began.

Today hanami is Japan’s favourite way to welcome spring. Friends and colleagues spread out a meal beneath trees in full bloom. And when darkness falls, the story goes on: in some places the trees are lit up for night viewing, known as yozakura.

Four addresses for an unforgettable hanami

The spots JNTO lists among Japan’s famous hanami places are enough to sketch a spring route: Ueno Park and the Meguro River area in Tokyo, Osaka Castle Park in Osaka and, in the north, Hirosaki Park in Aomori.

Hirosaki has a story of its own. Described as one of Japan’s three largest cherry blossom viewing spots, the park turns pink between late April and early May. Around 2,600 trees, more than 50 varieties, and trunks over 300 years old…

The secret lies in apples. The park’s groundskeepers use the pruning techniques of Aomori’s apple growers on the cherry trees, so each branch carries far more blossoms than usual. When the trees and Hirosaki Castle are lit up at night, the scene takes on a whole new character.

Not as a visitor, but like a local

During hanami you quickly find yourself part of the crowd. Knowing a few unwritten rules makes the experience genuinely local. Here is what JNTO recommends:

  • Finding a spot: Seating is first come, first served. Taking more space than you need, or someone else’s spot, is considered very rude.
  • Rubbish: There are few public bins in Japan. The rule is simple: take your rubbish home.
  • The trees: Cherry trees belong to the parks and grounds they grow in. Breaking off branches or picking flowers is never acceptable.
  • Getting there: Popular spots get crowded during the season. Taking public transport to the nearest stop and walking from there is the easiest option.
  • Clothing: Evenings turn cool. If you are heading out for night viewing, bring a warm layer.

Cherry blossom dates change every year and the most popular spots get very crowded. Check the latest bloom forecasts and official announcements for your destination shortly before you travel.

The sakura season is short. Perhaps that is exactly what makes it so precious. Catch the right moment, respect local customs, and a few hours under a tree can become the moment you remember most from your trip.
