Hallstatt: The Town Built by Salt

The salt story of Hallstatt, whose name means “salt settlement”: Bronze Age beginnings, the European culture that took its name, and its rebirth after the fire of 1750, based on UNESCO sources.

November 12, 20265 min readLast checked: November 12, 2026

Can a town’s name tell its fate? In Hallstatt, it can. According to UNESCO, the name means “salt settlement” and testifies to the town’s primary function. Behind the elegant lakeside houses and the church spire leaning against the mountain lies a story that began thousands of years ago. Its hero is a mineral: salt.

A treasure in the shadow of the mountains

Hallstatt lies in the Salzkammergut, part of the Eastern Alps. UNESCO describes this landscape as one of visual drama, with huge mountains rising abruptly from narrow valleys.

But these mountains are not just scenery. The region’s prosperity since medieval times has been based on salt mining around the town of Hallstatt. In UNESCO’s words, people have lived in the valleys between these huge mountains for over three millennia.

A craft that began in the Bronze Age

The story goes back a very long way. According to UNESCO, systematic salt production was carried out in the region as early as the Middle Bronze Age, in the late 2nd millennium BC. The method was simple: natural brine was captured in vessels and evaporated.

Underground salt mining began at the end of the Late Bronze Age.

Did you know? When mining resumed in the 8th century BC, archaeological evidence revealed a flourishing, stratified and highly organised Iron Age society with wide trade links across Europe. Today that culture carries the town’s name: the Hallstatt Culture.

Mining continued in Roman times and was revived in the 14th century. The mines and the evaporation of salt needed large amounts of timber, taken from the extensive upland forests that, from the 16th century, were controlled and managed directly by the Austrian Crown.

The town that rose from the ashes

The Hallstatt you photograph today is in fact the product of a second birth. In 1750, a fire destroyed the town’s timber buildings. According to UNESCO, the town was rebuilt afterwards in the late Baroque style.

The inside of the mountain changed over time too. UNESCO names the way mining has transformed the interior of the mountain as one of the exceptional aspects of this landscape.

Discovered by writers and painters

For centuries a town of salt, Hallstatt had its beauty “discovered” through other eyes in the early 19th century. According to UNESCO, writers such as the novelist Adalbert Stifter and the dramatic poet Franz Grillparzer discovered this Alpine landscape, and most of the leading paintings of the Biedermeier school took it as their subject.

Tourists followed them, which led to hotels and brine baths being built for visitors.

One more detail: the high pastures have been used for the summer grazing of sheep and cattle since prehistoric times. According to UNESCO, this seasonal migration still gives the valley communities rights of access to specific grazing areas today.

More than a landscape

UNESCO inscribed the Hallstatt-Dachstein / Salzkammergut Cultural Landscape on the World Heritage List in 1997. It highlights a continuing evolution covering 2,500 years, linked from the very beginning to the economic history of salt extraction.

According to UNESCO, this cultural landscape still retains the traces of salt mining, timber production, seasonal grazing and dairy farming. During your visit, respect this living heritage and the daily lives of the people who live here.

Next time you look at that famous lakeside view of Hallstatt, you won’t just see a pretty town. You will see more than three thousand years of work, and the story of a mining town that gave its name to a European culture.
