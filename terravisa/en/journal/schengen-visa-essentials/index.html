The Schengen Visa: What to Know Before You Apply

Schengen visa types, the 90/180-day rule, where and when to apply, required documents, fees and processing times, based on European Commission sources.

November 8, 20266 min readLast checked: November 12, 2026

Three days in Paris, four in Rome, a week in Barcelona. However free the route looks on paper, in the Schengen area it all runs on the same clock. Knowing how that clock works, and the fine print of the application, turns the trip you are dreaming of into one without surprises.

If you are planning a short trip to Europe and your passport requires a visa, the document you are looking for is most likely a Schengen visa. Below, we map out the way step by step, based on official European Commission information. One note: details can vary by consulate, and the consulate you apply to always has the final word.

The number behind it all: 90/180

A Schengen visa allows non-EU nationals to make a short, temporary visit of up to 90 days in any 180-day period to a country in the Schengen area.

Keep these two numbers in mind, because the whole plan revolves around them. The visa comes in three forms:

  • Single-entry visa: you enter the Schengen area once.
  • Multiple-entry visa: you can come and go several times for as long as the visa is valid.
  • Airport transit visa: lets you pass through the international transit area of a Schengen airport during a stopover, but not step outside it.

Whether your passport needs a visa is set by the EU’s official list. Curious about your own situation? Just choose your passport on TerraVisa’s country pages.

Which consulate opens the door?

If your route spans several countries, the first question is: where do you apply? The rule is actually clear:

  • Visiting more than one Schengen country: apply at the consulate of the country where you will spend the longest time.
  • Stays of equal length: the consulate of the first country you will enter is your address.
  • As a general rule, you apply at the consulate responsible for the country where you are legally resident.

The calendar is your best friend

Timing is one of the most important parts of the application. Submit it at least 15 days before your trip and no earlier than 6 months beforehand. Remember that you may need to book an appointment first; opening the calendar early makes everything easier.

What goes in your file?

The European Commission lists these core documents:

  • A valid passport that expires at least 3 months after you leave the Schengen area. For multiple-entry visas, this is counted from your departure from the last country you visit.
  • A visa application form.
  • A photo that meets ICAO standards.
  • Travel medical insurance covering emergency medical care, hospitalisation and repatriation, including in the event of death.
  • Supporting documents showing the purpose of your trip, your financial means, your accommodation and your intention to return home.

Your fingerprints are also collected when you apply (some applicants are exempt). The consulate may ask for additional documents if needed.

The cost and the wait

The fees are clear:

  • €90 for adults
  • €45 for children aged 6–12

In some countries, visa service centres collect applications on behalf of consulates and may charge an additional service fee. For certain applicants, the visa fee may be waived altogether.

And the wait… The normal processing time is 15 days. It can be extended to up to 45 days if a more detailed examination or additional documents are needed. If your application is refused, you will be told why and how to appeal.

The 90/180-day rule is calculated as a single period across all the Schengen countries you visit. The EU’s Entry/Exit System (EES) records your entries and exits electronically, so always keep the rule in mind when planning your dates. We explain how the system works at the border in our EES and ETIAS article.

A small exception on the map

Ireland and Cyprus are EU members but apply their own national entry rules. If either is on your route, remember to check that country’s official sources separately.

Visa rules and fees can change over time. Before you apply, take one more look at the current pages of your consulate and the European Commission. The rest is enjoying the journey.
